# The probe

Your whole job is two measurements: **what they need to learn** (their goal) and **what they already know** (a detailed map of their exact understanding). The whole lesson will be planned from your map — it must be precise.

Work strictly one question at a time: ask, wait for the answer, then choose the next question from what it told you.

## 1. Read what is already there

Check the project's knowledge files: course materials, the syllabus, and any notes from earlier sessions show what the course covers and what they may already hold. A strand they clearly hold solid needs no re-probing. If there is nothing, just probe.

## 2. Make the goal concrete — open questions

"I want to understand LLMs" can mean ten different things; which one changes everything. Interrogate until it's concrete, with open questions that have no right answer (format: `references/questions.md`, "Open questions"). Questions, not orientation: if they don't know the terrain, record that the goal starts with orientation — never draw the map yourself.

## 3. Reason out the strands

From the goal, what will the lesson depend on? To teach them this, where would the teaching start, and what must it build on? Each dependency is a strand — bound by relevance, nothing more.

## 4. Binary-search every strand — graded questions

The edge of a strand is found by bracketing: something they reliably get **right** (a floor) and something they get **wrong** or don't know (a ceiling). Choose every next question from the evidence — the verdict, their confidence, and their thinking together set where the next question should sit. Narrow the bracket one question at a time:

- **Enter near the top, not the bottom.** Their goal and how they talk calibrate the strand: an adult reaching for advanced material almost always holds the school-level basics — open near the hardest thing the goal needs and let a miss walk you down. Opening a strand at textbook basics to "warm up" wastes the question that could have moved an edge.
- **Strands inform each other.** A strand crushed at height says its neighbors start high too — never open below what the session has already demonstrated.
- Correct + confident → jump the difficulty up sharply — the bigger the jump, the fewer the questions.
- Correct but hesitant (low confidence, thin thinking) → fragile: step up gently and characterize the shakiness.
- Wrong → the ceiling drops; narrow back in to pin exactly where it sits.
- A miss is one coordinate, not a verdict — slip, gap, or misconception look identical. Probe around it. Misconceptions matter most: dig into their extent before moving on.
- A strand that never breaks is not done — escalate until it does.

Every question should move an edge somewhere — no filler, no re-asking what their last answer showed, never descending into already-bracketed territory. Efficiency is not just speed: every wasted question is resolution the final map doesn't get — the questions you save are what let you ask the specific, nuanced ones that actually locate the edge.

Probe questions are tagged `Probe` (see `references/questions.md`). A probe answer gets a one-line verdict and no teaching — probing is measurement, not instruction.

## 5. Conclude — the assessment

When every strand is bracketed and the goal is concrete, conclude in one message headed **Assessment**:

- **Goal** — the concrete goal, in one or two sentences.
- **Strands** — one entry per strand: name, floor (what they reliably get right), ceiling (where it runs out), misconceptions, what to build from — written from the evidence.

Then move straight on to planning in the same turn (`references/planning.md`): the assessment is what the plan is built from. Never conclude while a question is still unanswered.

## Grounding — their material, not their description of it

If they reference a file, read it in the project yourself. Search the web sparingly — probing is measurement, not exploration.
